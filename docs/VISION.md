# Vision — Signal

## One sentence
Signal is an immersive top-down 2D urban RPG about making a precarious living in one layered city: meet people, trade goods, work legitimate jobs, commit crimes, uncover secrets, take risks, and return to a hideout that gradually becomes more useful and personal.

## Genre and player fantasy
Signal blends:
- **Urban life and hustle simulation:** the player moves through a compact but explorable city, performs many small activities for money or resources, carries limited supplies, takes calculated risks, and lives with losses.
- **Crime and legitimate livelihoods in the same world:** the player can mix lawful work, trade, services and production with theft, fraud, fictional drug-related activity and other illegal opportunities. These are not separate maps, districts or character classes by default.
- **Narrative-driven RPG:** locations, characters, relationships, secrets and authored events give context and human stakes to the player's daily struggle.
- **Hands-on immersive interaction:** important activities happen through places, people and objects in the world, not only through abstract menus.

The player is not primarily operating a shop. A shop or counter may be one activity among many. The wider loop is going out, meeting people, doing things, trading or acquiring goods, deciding what to keep or risk, discovering what is happening around them, and returning to a hideout that gradually becomes more useful and personal.

## Core pillars
1. **A lived-in hideout:** a small house or planque that the player physically inhabits. Walk around it, use furniture, store belongings, decorate it, and return to it between outings.
2. **A shared, layered city:** legitimate businesses, ordinary residents, criminal opportunities and hidden dealings coexist in the same streets and buildings. No separate "legal city" and "underground city" is required.
3. **People with more than one side:** a person may have an ordinary public role and a concealed private activity. A baker might also deal in illicit goods; a neighbour might be a customer, supplier or fence. The player should not automatically know everyone's secrets.
4. **Goods with histories and qualities:** products can differ by quality, condition, authenticity, source, ownership/provenance and risk. Water, food, electronics, clothing, household goods and illicit products can all support interesting trade or activity loops.
5. **Many small, connected activities:** buying, selling, inspecting, collecting, repairing, processing, delivering, stealing, producing, helping, investigating and socialising should combine into larger player-driven sessions.
6. **Risk, loss and pressure:** decisions can cost time, money, goods, trust or safety. Criminal activity can carry harsh consequences; legitimate activity is generally lower-risk but should still have meaningful trade-offs.
7. **People, relationships and discovery:** NPCs have motives, routines, needs, relationships and secrets. The player can learn about opportunities through conversation, observation, rumours, requests and consequences.
8. **Progression through place and possessions:** improve the hideout with furniture, equipment, storage and decoration. Upgrades should be visible in the world.

## Reference games: what to learn from
| Reference | Take | Leave |
|---|---|---|
| Schedule I | A compact city loop, customers and contacts, returning to a base, visible progression | Copying its exact economy, scale or progression |
| Drug Dealer Simulator | Physical-feeling workspaces, preparation and storage interactions, atmosphere and pressure | Its specific systems or presentation copied literally |
| Probably Stolen | Goods with grades/quality, inspection and appraisal, customer budgets, trade-offs and risk attached to merchandise | Treating the whole game as a shop simulation or requiring a storefront as the core loop |
| Torn Crimes 2.0 | Varied crime categories, activity-specific prerequisites and outcomes, broad criminal progression inspiration | Copying its exact crime rules or turning every crime into an isolated menu action |
| Stardew Valley | Readable top-down spaces, approachable visual language, expressive interiors, decoration and a world that feels lived in | Farming as a required core loop, or copying its exact art style |
| Stalker / Tarkov | Scarcity, inventory pressure, meaningful loot choices and risk | Shooter-first gameplay or realism for its own sake |

These are reference points for experience and interaction quality, not templates to reproduce.

## First playable foundation: the hideout and its surrounding block
The first target is deliberately small:
- A compact, walkable outdoor city block.
- A few visible buildings with basic enter/exit transitions; interiors may initially be simple.
- One small starter house/hideout, built as a real walkable interior.
- A readable set of furniture and props: at minimum a bed, table, chair, storage and workbench.
- Basic object interaction with clear prompts and feedback.
- A workbench interaction that transitions into a dedicated visual close-up workspace and back to the same room.
- A visual style pass that makes the hideout feel inhabited rather than like a debug room.

The hideout is the emotional anchor. The city gives it context and a reason to leave.

## Visual direction
Top-down 2D with the readability and warmth of a game like Stardew Valley, adapted to a gritty contemporary urban setting. Prioritise clear silhouettes, readable rooms, expressive props, layered interiors, restrained neon accents and atmospheric lighting. The hideout should feel modest, personal and lived-in — not like a sterile management interface.

## Design principles
- Build connected player experiences, not isolated systems.
- Keep legal and illegal activities in the same social, physical and economic world.
- Do not label whole locations or NPCs as "underground" by default; concealed activity is something the player may discover.
- Important information can be uncertain or hidden. Let the player learn through observation, conversation, inspection, rumours and consequences.
- Give goods meaningful properties when useful: quality, condition, authenticity, origin/provenance and ownership status. Avoid creating complexity without player-facing value.
- Prefer world objects and spatial interactions when they strengthen immersion.
- Simulation should create meaningful pressure; narrative should make the pressure matter.
- Progression should be visible in places, equipment, relationships and possessions.
- Keep the first playable area small and coherent. Add scale only when the current space is enjoyable.
- New detailed gameplay rules beyond the approved direction remain proposals until their player-facing behavior is agreed.

## Not the first slice
A full drug economy, complex police simulation, exhaustive NPC simulation, faction warfare, multiple districts, a large quest structure, or a complete progression/equipment economy. These may become part of Signal later, but they do not block making the first house and city block feel good.


## Direction B emphasis — everyday livelihood (approved 2026-11-21)

A major player fantasy is building a modest, practical livelihood in the neighbourhood. The player can operate a production workshop from the hideout, make authored physical goods, fulfil a limited number of authored customer contracts, earn clean CARD and invest in practical workspace improvements. Legal work and crime remain parallel activities in the same shared city; neither path locks the player out of the other. The first production milestone is a player-operated hideout workshop, while the long-term content model remains compatible with NPC-owned production businesses. Detailed recipes, products, contract terms and upgrade costs require separate approval.
